Public Records and Their Importance

We've been quiet at DeFlock Joplin for a while, but we have still been doing research about Flock behind the scenes using public records, which is what want to talk about now. We have new records from a Flock camera at 4th and Maiden Lane to discuss, but we feel like we have lay some groundwork about why these records are significant first.

When the government creates any sort of record, they create it in our name with our taxes. Those records belong to the public.The public's right to inspect, copy, and redistribute those records is vital to a functional democracy. Voters must be able to know what their government is doing and the records request process is one way that is possible. Viewing records allows voters to make informed statements to their representatives and also make informed choices when casting votes.

Public records are so important that people have endured legal persecution fighting for public access to them. In 2008 Aaron Swartz, a computer programmer and activist, downloaded millions of documents from the federal court case system, PACER. He then uploaded them to a site that allowed others to access them free of charge. These documents make up our laws and are in a way the "operating system" of our government. Once laws are written, the courts must interpret them. Swartz and others felt that it was wrong to hide this data behind a paywall. It had also been demonstrated that the government was profiting off of PACER, despite the law saying PACER could only recoup its costs with fees. The liberated data was quickly used for public interest. Activists demonstrated privacy violations were occurring within the documents. This kind of privacy audit would not have been feasible under PACER's standard fee structure and the DOJ clearly was not doing the work.

The FBI surveilled Aaron Swartz over this incident, but no charges were filed because the FBI rightly concluded the data was public domain. However, a court precedent was not set. Swartz would later hang himself in 2013 after being over-zealously charged by the federal government for downloading large numbers of scientific articles with MIT's access in a separate incident. The scientific research these articles represent is often heavily, if not entirely, funded by taxpayers. Yet for-profit publishers are allowed to hold the body of human knowledge behind yet another paywall.

We bring Aaron Swartz up because his view on public records mirrors some of our own and one of the court cases we are going to talk about establishes what he did with PACER as a legal precedent.

Public Records and The Courts

The courts have established that public records and facts cannot be copyrighted in two court cases we will discuss below.

The first case affirms in law what Swartz did with PACER. Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc. (2020) involved a company that copied public records and made them available. Public.Resource.Org published the Official Code of Georgia Annotated online for free. The State of Georgia claimed copyright over the annotations made by legislators. These annotations are explanations of the law as written and should belong to the public. The Supreme Court ruled in favor of Public.Resource.Org. In the opinion the court said that statutes, regulations, and judicial opinions are "official edicts" that cannot be copyrighted. This also extends to annotations made by officials in their official capacity. The court ruled the public has a right to access and reproduce these public records, which includes use for commercial purposes.

The second case involves a telephone service and a publisher, Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co. (1991). The company Rural Telephone Service published a phone directory that included white pages contact listings. Feist Publications copied these listings to create a competing directory. Rural Telephone Service sued Feist for copyright infringement. The Supreme Court ruled unanimously against Rural Telephone Service. In the opinion the court said this is because facts cannot be copyrighted. A mere compilation of facts lacks the original creative expression required for copyright protection. With this foundation about public records and copyright in place, we can continue to Flock.

Flock Images are Public Record

As we said at the top, we recently obtained images from the Flock camera at 4th and Maiden Lane. Contrary to some other records we have requested, these were surprisingly easy to obtain, but perhaps some of the most important. We have been trying to determine if Joplin uses live video viewing that Flock released as a free update last year. We believe that they produced these records easily in an attempt to show they aren't using that feature. We still don't know for sure if Joplin has turned on the feature. They gave us no videos, but they also won't show us any record that indicates the feature's current status. Live view is [a tertiary concern?] actually besides the point here anyways.

We requested these images as a proof of concept and because we believe they are public nature by their nature:

  • They are collected by the government using taxpayer money.
  • The cameras generating the records are mounted in public spaces, on public infrastructure, and recording public areas.

As we have said, we are strong supporters of public records law and public records by their nature belong to the public. Nothing we are about to say should be taken as a criticism of public records law. If anything, we have ideas on how to improve records law that have nothing to do with this these images or the privacy implications. However, we have to point out the serious concerns a surveillance system like this has in our system and the supposedly transparent democracy we have.

Now that we have demonstrated anyone can obtain these images, we want to remind the reader that there is no license restricting these images. There is no restriction that prohibits the use of public records in a commercial product. The images contain no original work or expression. They are mere recordings of fact: A vehicle passed by "X" camera at "Y" time. This implies that the images are free to do with as the recipient pleases. Use your imagination to see all the ways this might be a problem. Here are a few we have imagined for you.

Corporate Surveillance Using Public Records

Corporations are hungry for data on their customers and users. Companies are now surveilling you within their stores to analyze your behaviors, interests, and habits. Companies collect signals from your electronic devices to help track you as you move through the store. If you've ever used a credit card and supplied a zip code, the company has probably narrowed down exactly who you are. As you exit, they are likely able to link you to a specific vehicle. Lowe's, Home Depot, tourist destinations, and others are all beginning to hire Flock.

For many companies, the data they collect about their users is the actual product as is the case with Google and Meta. For others, they spend resources and money to collect this data and link it to their customers.

Now imagine that Lowe's, Walmart, or another company wants to learn more than the little data they glean from interacting with you. Various corporations and data brokers could start buying up Flock records from local governments. This would allow them to analyze consumer behavior well beyond the store or online shopping activity. Then the companies would be able to send you ads based on what you do during your other activities in the real world. There are no laws on this data so the company and their employees can use it how they like. For a company with resources, it would be trivial to make software that ingests the images from the Flock cameras and builds trails on people just like Flock can. Data brokers will likely expand their business model to include collecting these records.

Criminal Surveillance Using Public Records

The same way that corporations gain value from this data, criminals can too. Criminals will be able to determine patterns of life so they may target their victims more effectively. Stalkers will be able to determine locations and routes their victims frequent. Burglars will be able to tell when property owners are away from home regularly.

The FBI has claimed that the logs published on HaveIBeenFlocked.com risk officer safety because it reveals their work schedules based on logged searches. The FBI accidentally makes the point that lots of data collected for one purpose can build a picture for a completely different one. Given the concern over this website's data, we can only assume that the FBI and local police will be very concerned that criminals could use the data directly from Flock cameras to determine patrol routes, patterns, and work schedules.

Can Flock Directly Use Camera Recordings?

With all of this out of the way, the elephant in the room is Flock. Although Flock signs a contract that says customers (the government) own the data, if it's public record the data cannot be owned in this way. If the government owns the data, isn't that just a roundabout way of saying the tax payers own it? If public record belongs to everyone, that means the recordings from Flock cameras belong to Flock too.

As such, it would seem this data is not protected and all the theater Flock goes through in the contracts is meaningless. Since this data is public record, does Flock even need to request it from Joplin before they begin using it? Or can they use it directly from their servers at will? They could perform analysis on the data far beyond what they claim they are doing and then sell that data to others. Since the data is public record, there is not much anyone could do about it beyond not collecting the data to begin with.

Police Considerations

There are other things we found in the pictures that may be concerning to supporters of this system, such as the police. In the images produced to us there are numerous, easy to find examples where a car is in an image where another car's plate is read, but there are no images for the car in question. This seems to imply that Flock did not capture a read on these plates. This happens when multiple cars go through the frame at once. In addition, the quality of the images is such that the oncoming plates are often completely illegible. Others, you can only "read" because you have the text from the camera to go off of.

These both raise concerns about the effectiveness of the system. For the first, imagine a scenario where an investigator requests images of every car that passed by a camera so they can investigate a nearby crime. If the police weren't aware of the nuances, they might believe every car has a unique image attached to it when they do not. An investigator might miss vehicles altogether. We concede it is possible that Flock has post processing that picks these plates up in the cloud, but if that's the case we think Flock would be generating a new image for the plate once it is found by the processing software.

The other concern is that the images are often not high enough quality for a human to verify the plate read.This could raise evidentiary implications in cases where Flock's images are used as evidence.

Conclusion

We have now demonstrated another critical problem with the implementation of a surveillance product like this. Residents, voters, city employees, and elected officials need to consider how this applies to other surveillance products The City is considering or has already purchased. The City has exposed residents and visitors of this city to enormous privacy risks that have likely not been considered. This exposure is compounded by the misuse incident acknowledged by The City on 1/102026, by there being no council vote, and by the lack of any notice that we were being opted into this system.

After learning their cameras were public record, agencies in Washington state began turning off their Flock cameras. We strongly encourage Joplin and other Missouri agencies to do the same.
